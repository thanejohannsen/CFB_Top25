Methodology

Five stages, every weight in one config file, and nothing hidden. Back to the rankings.

The short version

Rank everyone on three things: how good their record is for the schedule they played, what the betting market thinks they are worth on a neutral field, and how they have actually moved the ball once you adjust for who they played. Adjust for what their losses look like. Then shuffle the order so the ranking contradicts as few head-to-head results as possible — weighing how convincing each result was against how far a team would have to move from where its resume put it.

Stage 1 — The record, the market, and the play-by-play

base = w_sor × SoR_rank + w_market × Mkt_rank + w_perf × PPA_rank

All three arrive as national ranks where 1 is best, so the score is a weighted rank average and lower is better.

  • Strength of Record — how hard this record was to earn, computed here rather than taken from ESPN. Walk a reference team (the 25th best on the board) through the schedule this team actually played, and ask how often it would finish with at least this many wins. Texas opening 4–0 with Ohio State and Tennessee on the card scores 2.6% — about one time in forty. A 5–0 against nobody scores far higher. Every game and its probability is published in each team's panel.
  • Market rating — the neutral-field rating implied by betting lines. A point spread prices a matchup, so it is decomposed into one number per team: expected home margin = rating(home) − rating(away) + home field. The result is in points and reads directly — a team at +19.5 would be favoured by four over a team at +15.5 on neutral ground. Home field is measured from the lines rather than assumed, and comes out at about +2.4 points.
  • Play-by-play — points added per play on offence less points allowed on defence, garbage time stripped out, then adjusted for opponent strength the same way. This is the eye test, counted: it does not care whether the ball bounced your way at the end.

Why these two quality signals and not one

Because they know different things, and the pair beats either alone. Taking the 2025 season, ranking only on what was knowable at the time and then predicting the winner of every later game, the market alone got 61.2% of ranked-vs-ranked games right and the play-by-play alone 62.8% — while blending them 50/50 got 67.3%. The market prices injuries, suspensions and quiet improvement that no box score shows; the play-by-play knows how a team actually played regardless of what the closing line said.

The split between the record and the quality signals, though, is a judgement and not a measurement. Predicting games is not what a resume is for, so no backtest can set it. It sits at half and half, and the cost of that choice is visible: on the completed 2025 season a 7–6 Penn State lands at 24th, because its record was ordinary but the market rated it 13th and the play-by-play 16th.

Why the resume is computed here and not taken from ESPN

It tracks ESPN's Strength of Record closely at the top, so this is not a change of opinion. It is a change of provenance. ESPN serves that number from one undated snapshot, which means on a finished season it describes the finished season — so no backtest could ever check it, and the evaluation tool had to leave it out and score half the formula. Betting lines are stamped per game. Computing the resume from them is what finally lets the whole ranking be graded.

It also means the number is inspectable. The curve from a point spread to a win probability is a normal distribution with a standard deviation of 13.5 points — a 14-point favourite wins about 85% of the time, a 30-point favourite about 99%. Getting that curve wrong is quiet and expensive: an earlier draft used a logistic at the same scale, priced a 30-point favourite at 90%, and made every resume in the country look harder to earn than it was.

Why FPI is no longer in the formula

It used to be the quality term, and two things pushed it out. The market and the play-by-play simply do its job better. And FPI cannot be honestly checked: the feed serves one snapshot with no week dimension, so grading a ranking built from it against a finished season is reading the answer key. An earlier version of this project quoted 75.5% accuracy from exactly that mistake. Betting lines and per-game play-by-play are both stamped per game, so a cutoff is a real cutoff.

FPI is still here, in the two places where one number has to settle something: as the last tiebreak when two teams score identically, and in weighing which head-to-head result to set aside when no market rating exists for either team.

Why Strength of Schedule is not in the formula

It used to be, at a quarter of the weight, and it was the single biggest source of nonsense. Strength of Record already accounts for the schedule — it means "how good is your record given who you played". Adding schedule strength again counts it twice, and worse, rewards playing hard games whether or not you win them.

The damage was not subtle. A 3–2 Clemson sat at 16th on the strength of its schedule alone, while a 5–0 Georgia with the country's second-best rating sat at 23rd for having played an easy one. Measured against the AP poll across two seasons, dropping the term moved rank correlation from τ +0.33 to +0.52 on the current season and +0.62 to +0.77 on the last completed one.

Schedule strength is still shown for every team, and the weight is still a knob in the config for anyone who wants it. It just no longer moves the ranking.

Stage 2 — Resume adjustment

Strength of Record knows who you played but not how you lost. A three-point road loss to the eventual best team in the country and a 24-point home loss to a middling one can produce the same SoR. They are not the same result, so four adjustments move the base score:

  • Loss quality — for each defeat, half the margin once venue is accounted for, half the weakness of whoever beat you.
  • Best win — how good the best team you beat was.
  • Game control — the feed's average in-game win probability rank: did you lead comfortably, or survive?
  • Against the number — how the team played compared with what the market expected of it, game by game.

Against the number

A resume says who you beat. It cannot say whether you looked like you meant it. The market posts, before kickoff, how much a team should win by; the difference between that and what happened is the cleanest statement of "did they play well" the data offers.

The asymmetry falls out of the arithmetic rather than needing a rule. Beating a bad team by 17 when you were favoured by 28 is a miss. Beating a good team by 1 when you were favoured by 3 is par. The easier a game was supposed to be, the more a flat performance costs you — and a team whose close wins came against good opponents loses almost nothing.

This is what separates two unbeaten teams. In week 5 of 2026, Alabama were +14.1 per game against the number and Notre Dame +0.8, having failed to cover against Michigan State by 12 and at North Carolina by 10 — and that, not their record, is why Notre Dame is not first.

It can only ever be a modifier. Ranked on its own, this measure puts Georgia State, James Madison and New Mexico at the top of the country, because it measures exceeding expectations rather than being good — a bad team losing by 10 as a 20-point underdog covers. It adjusts a resume that already knows who you beat, and it is deliberately kept small enough not to behave like a ranking term of its own.

Each is a z-score scaled into rank points, so the adjustment is measured against how teams actually vary this season rather than an arbitrary constant.

Stage 3 — Upset regression

When a result spans a wide gap — say the 38th-best resume beats the 5th — it is evidence that both teams were mis-rated, not just one. So both are pulled toward the midpoint of their scores and the later stages sort them out from there. The winner is not teleported to the top, and the loser does not get to pretend the game never happened.

The pull is deliberately partial. At full strength the stage fights stage 4, which simply drags the winner back where it started, and the result swings wildly from week to week for no gain.

Stage 4 — Weighted head-to-head

cost(order) = strength × Σ weight(overridden result) + drift_weight × Σ |position − base position|drift_exponent

Head-to-head is a preference, not an absolute rule. The ranking is the ordering that minimises the cost above, so the results it ends up contradicting are by construction the least convincing ones. Each result's conviction weight comes from four signals:

It is a lighter touch than it looks, deliberately. The résumé already contains these results — it is a list of games with win probabilities, so beating a good team is counted there first. Swap Ole Miss's win over LSU for a cupcake at the same record and their résumé falls from 30.2% to 63.1%: the win is worth 2.1× before this stage begins. Forcing the same result hard here would charge for it twice. So this stage settles conflicts the résumé left open rather than enforcing results from scratch — and with it switched off entirely, 20 of 27 results still come out honoured on their own.

  • Location-adjusted margin — winning on the road is worth more than the same margin at home. A one-point home win scores negative: home field alone is worth more than the margin.
  • Rating gap — a result that contradicts the power ratings is weaker evidence that the winner is genuinely better. The gap is measured on the market's neutral-field points, falling back to FPI where the market has no opinion.
  • Recency — later games say more about current strength.
  • Common opponents — how the two fared against the teams they both played.

Why not simply enforce every result?

Because strict enforcement means sorting the head-to-head graph topologically, which holds a team behind every team its conqueror is behind. Schedule density tracks schedule strength, so that systematically sinks teams who play hard schedules and floats teams who play nobody. Tested on a real completed season it put three teams with bottom-third schedules in the top twelve while dropping the two best resumes to 17th and 20th.

Real head-to-head graphs are also not tidy triangles. One completed season produced a single tangle of 22 mutually entangled teams, where resolving each loop separately is not even computable. Weighing the results instead has neither problem, and a team nobody in the pool played simply stays where its resume put it.

What it costs to override a result

Every result carries a real price, and that is a deliberate correction. Conviction is a blend of z-scores, so it sits near zero and has to be shifted upward before it can serve as a cost. That shift used to be measured from the weakest result on the board, which meant whichever game happened to be least convincing was always pinned at a token value — about a seventeenth of a typical result — however real that game was. It is why a 2025 Oklahoma State win over Oregon was set aside essentially for free. The floor is now an absolute level of conviction: a weak result costs a full unit because it genuinely is weak, and a convincing one costs roughly three times that rather than seventeen.

The drift exponent is what keeps a lone result from carrying a team across the board: because long moves cost disproportionately more, how far a result can move a team scales with how convincing that result was. A marginal win buys a few places; an emphatic one buys many.

Stage 5 — Reporting contradictions

Loops are found with Tarjan's algorithm for strongly connected components. They are reported, not resolved — stage 4 already decided the order. Every loop and every overridden result is published on the front page, because “my team beat them and is still ranked below them” deserves a real answer.

Known limitations

  • Strength of Record, Strength of Schedule and FPI are ESPN metrics served through CollegeFootballData.com. They are third-party opinions and they move during the week.
  • The market rating knows things results cannot. A spread prices injuries, suspensions and weather, which is exactly why it predicts well — and it is also a departure from a pure resume ranking. A team can be rated highly here for reasons that never appear in a box score.
  • The market rating also leans on lines for the coming week, which is what makes it a current opinion rather than an average of stale pre-game guesses. A closing line for next Saturday has priced in every result so far and nothing after it, so this stays inside what is knowable — but it does mean the rating moves when the market's view of a future game moves.
  • A team with too few lined games gets no market rating at all, and is scored on the terms it has with the weights rescaled. Its row shows which input is missing. In practice every FBS-vs-FBS game in both seasons checked had a line, so this mostly affects the opening weeks.
  • Early in a season the schedule can split into groups that have not played each other. Ratings are only comparable inside such a group, and the snapshot says so in its warnings when it happens.
  • The ordering is found by local search. It is a strong local optimum, not a proven global one; in practice it reaches the same answer from any starting permutation of the inputs, but that is an observation rather than a guarantee.
  • A chain of individually reasonable moves can still add up to a large one: if A beat B, B beat C and C beat D, A can end up well above its resume position. The per-team drift is published so this is visible rather than hidden.
  • The FPI feed has no per-week endpoint, so a week's snapshot is taken when it is generated and earlier weeks cannot be reconstructed after the fact. The same is true of Strength of Record, which is why the backtesting tool refuses to score either of them and reports only on the market and play-by-play half.
  • Early in a season Strength of Record is volatile, and an undefeated team with a soft schedule can look better than it is.
